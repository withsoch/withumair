Most advice on how to write a linkedin about section tells you to tell your career story or fill in a template. Both produce the same thing: a tidy paragraph that could belong to anyone with your job title.

The missing step comes before the writing. You have to decide who the section is for and what that person should conclude after reading it. Wording is the easy part once that is settled.

Below is the order I work in, plus a test you can run on your own profile in an afternoon.

## What should a LinkedIn About section include?

A good About section says who you help, what problem you solve, why you are credible, and how to reach you. That is the whole job. Everything else, including career history and personal detail, only earns a place if it supports one of those four things.

The About section is the same thing as the Summary section, and many people still call it a LinkedIn summary. It can run to 2,000 characters, but the limit is a ceiling, not a target.

## Step 1: Who is this section actually for?

Most founders write for everyone and so land with no one. The section gets vague because the reader in mind is vague: clients, partners, recruiters, investors, old colleagues, all at once.

![Hand writing a single sentence in a notebook beside a laptop](/blog/write-linkedin-about-section-founders-inline-1.jpg)

*Pick one reader and write one sentence about what they should conclude. Every later line has to pass it.*

Pick one reader. Describe them the way you would describe a specific person: what they do, what they are stuck on, what they are weighing when they open your profile.

Then write one sentence: **after reading this, that person should think, "This is who I call for that problem."**

That sentence is your positioning statement. It is not copy to paste in. It is the filter every later line has to pass.

Here is why it comes first. Without it, you choose words by how they sound. With it, you choose them by whether they move that one reader toward that one conclusion.

If you serve several kinds of people, pick the one you most want more of. The others will still understand a clear profile. They will not understand a blurry one.

## Step 2: How do you write the first three lines?

Only the first three lines show before a visitor has to click "See more". Treat them as the entire section, because for many visitors they are.

![Person holding a smartphone and reading a profile on the screen](/blog/write-linkedin-about-section-founders-inline-2.jpg)

*Most visitors never get past the lines shown before See more, so those lines are the whole pitch.*

Reviewing founder profiles, I see the same two openings again and again. One starts with job history: "I have spent fifteen years in operations." The other starts with a mission: "I am passionate about helping businesses grow." Both spend the only visible space on something the reader cannot use.

The opening that earns the click names the reader's problem, in their terms. Compare these, as examples:

> Before: "Experienced founder with a passion for helping companies scale."
> After: "Most small agencies hit a ceiling when every client still depends on the founder. I help owners build a delivery team that does not."

The second tells the right reader they are in the right place. It also makes the wrong reader leave, which is fine.

Your headline carries search visibility, within its 220 characters. The About section does the persuading once someone is already looking at you, so do not stuff the opening with keywords.

## Step 3: How do you show you've built something real without bragging?

Swap adjectives for plain specifics. "Results-driven" asks the reader to trust you. "I built X for Y, and Z changed" lets them check.

The structure is simple: what you built, who it was for, what was different afterward. Use only things that are true and that you could explain on a call.

- **What you built.** Name the thing: a firm, a product, a process, a team.
- **Who it served.** The same kind of reader you chose in Step 1.
- **What changed.** Describe the change in plain terms. Add a figure only if you can stand behind it.

Many founders feel that stating this is boasting. It is not. Boasting is claiming a quality. Reporting what happened is information, and the reader is deciding with or without it.

If you cannot say what changed, that is useful to know before you write anything.

## Step 4: How should you structure and end it?

Keep the structure short and plain:

1. **Opening.** The reader's problem, in the first three lines.
2. **Proof.** What you have built and what changed.
3. **How you work.** A few sentences on your approach and who you are not for.
4. **Next step.** One clear action.

Write in first person. Most professionals prefer it because it feels more authentic and approachable, and third person reads like a press release you wrote about yourself.

Keep paragraphs to one to three sentences. Most people read on a phone, and dense blocks get skipped.

End with one instruction, such as "Message me with the problem you are facing" or a single place to book a call. Do not end on a string of keywords. A reader who has agreed with you needs a door, not a tag cloud.

Length follows from this. Credible guidance puts a summary at roughly 300 to 500 words at most, and a focused section usually runs well under that. Also remember the summary carries over if you download your profile as a resume, so it should hold up outside LinkedIn.

## How can you test your About section in an afternoon?

Find someone who does not know your work well. Show them only the first three lines, nothing else, and ask two questions: who is this for, and what does this person do?

![Two colleagues sitting at a table talking while looking at a laptop](/blog/write-linkedin-about-section-founders-inline-3.jpg)

*Show someone only the opening and ask who it is for. Their answer tells you what to fix.*

If they answer both correctly, the opening works. If not, make these edits in order:

- **Vague reader.** Go back to Step 1 and name a narrower person.
- **Vague offer.** Replace the category word ("consultant", "strategist") with the problem you solve.
- **Wrong first line.** Cut any job history or mission statement and start with the reader's problem.
- **Adjectives.** Replace each one with a fact about what you built.

Then repeat with a different person. Two clean answers in a row is a good sign.

## FAQ

### Is the summary and About section the same on LinkedIn?

Yes. LinkedIn's About section is also called the Summary, and people use the names interchangeably. It is the free-text area near the top of your profile where you explain who you help and why you are worth trusting.

### How long should a LinkedIn About section be?

The limit is 2,000 characters, which is about 300 to 500 words. Shorter is usually better. Use what you need to make the reader's problem, your proof and the next step clear, then stop.

### Should I write my LinkedIn About section in first or third person?

First person. Most professionals prefer it because it feels more authentic and approachable. Third person puts distance between you and the reader, which works against a founder whose main asset is being a real person who built something.
